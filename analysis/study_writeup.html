The Zero-Growth Floor

In an algal toxicity test, a high enough dose makes the population shrink and the growth rate goes negative. Common practice is to set those values to zero. This is what that does to the numbers you report — and why a better experiment makes it worse, not better.

The problem

Growth rate is a rate, and rates can be negative

An algal growth test measures how fast a population multiplies. You count cells at the start and again at the end, and the specific growth rate is the log ratio divided by the test duration. In the controls the population grows, so the rate is positive. At high toxicant concentrations the population can shrink — more cells die than divide — and the rate comes out negative. That is not an error or an artefact. It is the correct measurement of a population in decline.

But a negative growth rate is awkward. It looks wrong on a plot, it breaks a percent-of-control calculation, and several standard models cannot produce one. So laboratories routinely remove it: they replace the negative values with zero, or they constrain the fitted curve so its lower plateau cannot go below zero, or both. The question this study asks is simple — does that change the answer you report?

The answer matters because the reported answer is a regulatory number. The ErC50 is the concentration that halves the growth rate; the ErC10 is the concentration that reduces it by a tenth. These feed into environmental quality guidelines. If a data-handling convention shifts them, it shifts the concentration a regulator considers safe.

The six methods

What people actually do with the negative values

Six approaches were compared. Three leave the measurement intact; three impose the zero boundary in one way or another. Throughout the analysis files they are referred to by the short codes in the first column.

Code What it does The claim it makes about the data
A Analyse the growth rates as measured; let the model estimate how far the curve falls. The negative values are real measurements and are used as such.
C Declare each negative value as censored at zero — tell the model "this observation is at or below zero" without asserting a value. Truthful but deliberately less informative: keeps the direction, discards the magnitude.
D Cut the concentration series off where the fitted curve crosses zero and analyse only what remains. Nothing false, but the high-dose data are thrown away.
B1 Replace every negative growth rate with zero. Model unchanged. False. Asserts the population held steady when it was dying.
B2 Keep the data as measured, but force the curve's lower plateau to sit exactly at zero. False. Asserts no dose can ever reduce growth below zero.
B3 Both of the above — floor the data and pin the plateau. False, twice over. This is the most common combination in practice.

A note on method C. Censoring is the technique used elsewhere for values below a detection limit. Saying "this is at most zero" is a true statement that stops the model being dragged upward by a fabricated value. It is the method available to you when the negative values were never recorded in the first place — which is often the case with historical data.

How it was tested

Four real tests, and a simulation where the truth is known

The four real datasets are growth tests on two microalgal species — two on Cladocopium proliferum (7 days) and two on Rhodomonas salina (3 days). Between 14% and 30% of their observations sit at or below zero growth. Real data show whether the methods disagree, but they cannot show which one is right, because nobody knows the true ErC50 of a real toxicant.

So the core evidence is a simulation. Curves with a known true ErC10 and ErC50 were generated, realistic measurement noise added, and each simulated dataset analysed by all six methods. Because the truth is known, each method can be scored on two things that matter:

  • Bias — does the method systematically over- or under-estimate the true value?
  • Coverage — of the 95% credible intervals it produces, what fraction actually contain the truth? A well-behaved method gives 95%.

Twelve scenarios, 240 simulated datasets each, six methods: 17,280 model fits, with no worker failures and one scenario losing 5 of 240 iterations for a single method. The simulation deliberately generates data with noise that grows toward the bottom of the curve and fits a model that assumes constant noise, because that is the realistic situation rather than a flattering one.

Finding 1

Flooring pulls the ErC50 down by 5 to 9 per cent

Where the test design actually reaches into negative growth — about 15% of observations below zero, which is typical of the real datasets — the six methods separate cleanly.

Method ErC50 bias Coverage Reading
C — censor −0.1%0.83 Essentially unbiased
A — as measured +0.7%0.92 Essentially unbiased; best coverage
D — truncate −1.6%0.85 Acceptable, but discards data
B1 — floor data −4.8%0.70 Biased low; intervals miss 3 times in 10
B3 — floor + pin −6.4%0.61 Worse; intervals miss 4 times in 10
B2 — pin plateau −9.3%0.97 Worst bias. High coverage is not a virtue here — see Finding 4

The direction is consistent: flooring makes the toxicant look more potent than it is, because the curve is forced to turn upward at the bottom and the whole fit shifts to accommodate it. And the penalty grows the further the true curve descends below zero — method B1 moves from −2.5% to −5.5% to −6.3% as the true decline deepens, while methods A and C stay flat.

Finding 2

A more precise experiment does not make the bias go away

This is the result that settles the question. The simulation was run across four levels of measurement noise, holding everything else fixed. Reading left to right, the experiment gets more precise — the control variability falls from 9.6% to 1.9%.

If the disagreement between methods were just estimation error, every method would converge on the truth as precision improves. That is exactly what methods A, C and D do. The flooring methods do not: they level off at a fixed, non-zero distance from the true value.

Finding 3

The confidence intervals collapse onto the wrong answer

Bias alone understates the problem. A 95% credible interval is supposed to contain the true value 95% of the time. As the experiment gets more precise, the interval gets narrower — but under flooring its centre stays displaced. The interval shrinks around a wrong number.

At the highest precision tested, methods B1 and B3 contained the true ErC50 in 0 of 240 simulated datasets. Not a low rate. Zero.

Finding 4

The other failure looks like success

Method B2 — pinning the curve's plateau to zero while leaving the data alone — reaches 100% coverage. Every single interval contained the truth. That is not a good result. Its intervals are simply so wide that they contain almost anything, while its point estimate sits 11% low.

If coverage had been reported without a measure of interval width, the two worst methods would have scored as the two best. B2 would have looked perfectly calibrated at 1.00, and B1 and B3 would have looked merely imprecise rather than confidently wrong. Both readings are the opposite of the truth.

On the real datasets B2's intervals are 2.9 and 116 times wider than the intact analysis, and it was unusable for at least one endpoint on all four datasets — its interval either ran to the edge of the tested concentration range or spanned the entire design.

The real data

What this costs on an actual test

The simulation says which method is right. The four real datasets say how much it matters in practice. On the two datasets where B2 produced a usable ErC50 at all, it returned 74% and 43% of the value the intact analysis gave. The convention roughly halves the reported ErC50 on real data.

The clearest single illustration comes from a Rhodomonas salina test where the population fell below the counting limit at the highest doses. The lower plateau of the curve is the parameter most directly affected, and how you declare those observations decides what you get:

How the below-limit values were declared Plateau estimateInterval width What it means
Substituted at the detection limit −1.990.06 Confidently precise — and asserting a value nobody measured
Censored: "at or below the limit" −5.648.49 Honest: the data contain almost nothing about this parameter
Interval-censored between extinction and the limit −2.450.59 Usable again, but resting on the assumed extinction floor

Read the middle row carefully. Once the false assertion is withdrawn, the interval widens 160-fold and runs down to a rate at which the population would fall by a factor of e33 over the test. That is the prior talking, not the data. Nothing looks mechanically wrong — the chains mix, the convergence diagnostic is 1.00 — which is precisely why substitution is dangerous. It replaces an honest "we don't know" with a confident wrong number, and no routine diagnostic flags it.

The boundary condition

When none of this matters

All of the above applies only where the test design actually reaches into negative growth. Where the highest concentration stops at or before the point the curve crosses zero — fewer than about 4% of observations negative — the picture is completely different. There, the lower plateau is barely identified by any method, all six carry a shared bias of the same sign and similar size, and the flooring methods can even come out marginally ahead.

That is not evidence that flooring helps. It is the intact analysis carrying a baseline error, from a plateau the design never measured, that flooring happens to offset. Averaging the two situations together produces a number that describes neither. The single most useful diagnostic is the fraction of your observations at or below zero growth — if it is negligible, the convention costs you nothing; if it is 15% or more, it costs you the numbers above.

Recommendation

What to do instead

  • Analyse the growth rates as measured and let the model estimate the lower plateau. Nothing in a Gaussian concentration–response fit requires the response to be positive.
  • If the negative values were never recorded — a common situation with historical or third-party data — declare them as censored at zero. This recovers essentially all of what flooring loses, and it is available when re-analysing the raw values is not.
  • Do not convert to percent inhibition before fitting. That step caps the response at 100%, which is what pushes analysts toward a bounded model in the first place. The problem enters in data preparation, not in the model.
  • Report the fraction of observations at or below zero growth alongside the endpoint, so a reader can tell whether the question even arises for your dataset.
  • Never report interval coverage without interval width. Finding 4 is the reason.

Methods A and C are close enough to be treated as equivalent on the evidence here. The claim this study supports is not that one of them wins — it is that B1, B2 and B3 lose.

Honesty

What this study cannot claim

  • Neither recommended method reaches nominal coverage on ErC50. Method A holds 0.87–0.94 and method C 0.78–0.83 against a nominal 0.95. They are far better than the alternatives but they are not perfectly calibrated, and that should be stated rather than glossed.
  • The NSEC endpoint was withdrawn. It was going to be a third reported endpoint, but it is defined against the spread of the control response, so the true no-effect concentration is its limiting value rather than its expectation. At realistic noise its coverage against the true value falls to zero for every method including the intact one. Only method-to-method contrasts are meaningful for it.
  • The original hypothesis was untestable. The study set out to show that the damage scales with a quantity derived from the control growth rate. On the scale the endpoints are actually reported on, that quantity cannot affect them — the model is exactly invariant to it, verified numerically. The concentration axis simply does not involve it. The relevant axes turned out to be how far the design reaches past zero, and how precise the measurement is.
  • Four real datasets are close to powerless. They are consistent with the simulation, but they cannot order themselves by the predicted quantity, and no single one of them is decisive. The simulation carries the argument.
  • The plateau parameter is prior-sensitive even in the recommended methods. Widening its prior sixteenfold moves it by a factor of nearly five. It moves the reported ErC50 by at most 4.8%, against a 33% gap to the pinned method — so the conclusions hold — but the plateau itself should not be read as an estimate when the data do not reach it.
